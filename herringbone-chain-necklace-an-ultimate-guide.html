Herringbone Chain Necklace: An Ultimate Guide

A herringbone chain looks like a single ribbon of liquid metal. Here is how to choose the right width, length and metal, how to spot quality, and how to keep it from kinking.

By McGee Jewelry · Updated October 2026

What is a herringbone chain?

A herringbone chain is made of short, slanted links set tightly side by side in alternating directions, so the surface reads as a flat, zigzag ribbon. The name comes from the fishbone-like weave found in tweed fabric. Because the links lie flush against each other, the chain has a smooth, mirror-like face that catches light along its whole length.

Unlike a cable or rope chain, a herringbone sits flat on the skin instead of rolling. That flatness is its charm, and also its weak point, as the care section explains.

Why it never goes out of style

  • A clean collarbone line. It lies flat, so it looks sleek rather than busy.
  • Stands alone. One chain is a complete, minimalist statement.
  • A strong base layer. It anchors finer chains worn above or below it.
  • Light play. The flat surface reflects light, so even simple metals look luxurious.

Choosing width and length

Width sets the mood and length sets where the chain sits. Wider chains look shorter and stiffer on the neck, so many people go one length longer than they would with a fine chain.

Metal and finish

Herringbones come in solid gold (14k or 18k), gold-filled, gold vermeil, sterling silver and gold-plated versions. Because the whole surface is exposed and flexes constantly, thin plating wears quickly on this style. For daily wear, solid gold or sterling silver is the safer choice. A quality checklist

  • Drape. It should flow smoothly, with no stiff patches.
  • Flat lay. Set it on a table. It should lie flat without twisting.
  • Even links. Uniform size and spacing along the whole length.
  • Clasp. A sturdy lobster clasp with a soldered jump ring.
  • Hallmark. Look for stamps such as 14K, 18K or 925.
  • Solid or hollow. Ask. Hollow chains look larger for the price but dent more easily.

How to style it

A herringbone shines against open collars, V-necks and scoop necklines, where its flat surface is visible. Under a high neckline it disappears, so wear it long over the top instead.

With a pendant, choose a light one on a smooth slide bail. A heavy pendant pulls the chain and can start a kink. To layer, pair it with a finer chain about 4 to 6 cm away in length, and keep one metal dominant. Hoops and studs work best with it, and the earring finder can narrow the shape.

Caring for a herringbone

The flat, tightly set links mean a kink is hard to undo. Protect it like this:

  1. Never tug, twist or sleep in it.
  2. Fasten the clasp before storing, and keep it flat or hanging.
  3. Clean with warm soapy water and a soft brush, moving along the links. Pat dry flat.
  4. Keep it away from chlorine and take it off for workouts.

If it does kink, do not force it straight. A jeweler can sometimes ease a mild one. More steps are in the care guide.

Is it worth the price?

A herringbone usually costs more than a cable chain of the same weight because it takes more work to make. To spend less, choose a narrower width or sterling silver, and use the gold calculator to compare how much real gold you get. Save pieces you like to your wishlist.

Frequently asked questions

Can I wear a herringbone chain every day?

Yes, if it is solid gold or sterling silver. Take it off for sleep, workouts and swimming to reduce kinks and wear.

Can a herringbone hold a pendant?

A light pendant on a smooth slide bail usually works. Heavy pendants can pull on the flat links and cause kinks.

Which width should I choose?

Most people like 4 to 5 mm. Choose 2 to 3 mm for something delicate, or 6 mm and up for a bold statement.

Why does a herringbone kink?

The links are flat and tightly interlocked, so a sharp twist or pull can lock them out of line. Gentle handling and flat storage prevent most kinks.
